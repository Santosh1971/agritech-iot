GPSIOAM · Farm IoT workshop · Pre-workshop questionnaire

What the students told us

Before building anything, we asked the students about the farms they know: where farmers lose money, how they decide to spray and irrigate, why they don’t adopt technology, and what the students themselves want to build. This is an anonymised summary of their answers. Thank you for making the time for it.

10 of 11 students responded · 28 September 2026 · Numbers show how many students chose each option (more than one could be chosen).

The class

Close to the farm, and eager to build a business

How farmers decide when to spray

What this means: most spraying is a reaction to damage that is already visible. Temperature and humidity records can warn of disease risk days earlier, which means fewer, better-timed sprays. This is the core of the TH monitor project.

Pumps and water

Motors run dry, burn out and get stolen

Pump problems they have seen

  • Running dry7
  • Motor burnt7
  • Cable or starter theft5
  • Phase failure / low voltage3
  • Night trips to switch on3

In their words

“The pump is near the river. It’s horrible to switch it on at midnight.”
“Power comes at a fixed time and the farmer has to go to the farm to switch on the pump.”

Irrigation they know: drip (6), borewell (4), sprinkler (4), rain-fed only (4), flood, open well and canal.

What this means: students were unsure whether farmers would pay just to switch a pump from a phone. But dry running, burnt motors and theft are costly and common. So the pump project focuses on protecting the motor: confirm water is flowing, stop on dry run, and alert on tampering, with remote switching as a bonus.

Whom farmers trust for anything new

  • Input dealer9

  • KVK / university3
  • YouTube2
  • WhatsApp groups2
  • Agriculture officer1
  • Company representative0
“Seeing is believing.”
“Demonstrate and use the technology in his own language.”
“Free trials and showing results on the same crop.”

What this means: farmers adopt what a trusted dealer or neighbour has shown working on a real farm. Most of these students have family farms, so the devices they build can become demonstration units in their own villages. That is the most credible way to reach farmers, and the start of a real business.

Willingness to pay for a disease-risk device: 4 said ₹2,000–5,000, 4 said only if free, 2 were unsure.

Business ideas students named

  • Storage of agricultural commodities
  • Soil nutrient (NPK) testing
  • Disease detection in sericulture
  • Market risk and fair prices
  • Input supply and processing
  • Services for small landholders
Their goals

What they want from Thursday

“Proper technical and practical knowledge about the devices rather than theory.”
“How to make sensors, and how they work.”
“How to create an app for farmers.”
“Farm solutions with low-cost technology.”

The farm problems they want to solve

  • Pest and disease, detected early, before it is visible in the field

How we will use this on Thursday

  • Start from their problems. The day opens with these findings, not with our slides.
  • Teams around real uses: a disease-risk alert, a storage monitor for grain and commodities, and pump protection against dry running and theft.
  • Low cost first, with each team costing its device, because cost was the number one hurdle.
  • Market price: we discuss how farm data connects to better prices, through storage conditions and timing of sale.
  • Demonstration units: students take their devices home to show on their family farms.
